**Verdict:** useful casebook; unvalidated trading system. The caveats are candid, but the instructions outrun them.

**(a) Hindsight dressed as rules.** “Joining late is usually fine” and “the real errors were not entering and exiting too tight” are unsupported. The sample requires an eventual qualifying rise of at least 1.5x or 2x. Its “fraction still ahead” divides by the eventual peak's log gain, from a retrospectively selected base. It describes successful paths; it is neither the probability of profit nor the return available to today's buyer. “Multi-year” is also known afterwards. Of 1,558 breakout events, 25.6% subsequently touched another 50%; even that is a peak-touch rate, not a realised trading win rate.

The best antidote is already in the population: even among 248 finished winners, the 20% stop produced mean 1.25x, median 1.01x, with 49% losing; exact win rate and worst case are not reported. Costs and executable exits are missing. Wider stops hurt the shorter runs. Calling a 20% trail “too tight” optimises captured upside after choosing winners, not account survival before knowing them.

The named 25–40% stops, “third limit-down” exit, 3x cutoff and re-entries were selected after seeing paths. The deepest dip *so far* is observable if the base was frozen; surviving the completed path is hindsight. PRL and MACFL helped construct the reflex, so catching them proves fit, not prediction.

Survivorship is not simply “delisted stocks omitted”: 139 were loaded. It persists in selecting winning episodes and the survivor-heavy sector list; crude delisted adjustments and backfilled pre-2019 KMI membership weaken comparability. Sector labels use peer returns through the eventual peak, while some filing labels look forward. These are retrospective classifications. Eight agreeing winners do not establish an edge. “No type” does not identify the failed three quarters of breakouts; typed candidates can fail too.

**(b) What survives.** Types 1–3—regime, sector tide, crash reclaim—are credible contexts for a watchlist. Their exact entries, exits and expected returns remain unvalidated. Type 2 even borrows the multi-year group's 3.3x/387-session summary and the five-bagger group's 5.4x-ahead statistic; those are not sector-tide population statistics.

Types 4–5 offer useful accounting questions: temporary windfall versus recurring operating improvement. They do not establish that a low trailing P/E or fast price response predicts continuation. The fast-reply branch has two selected observations. Type 6 is one ISL and one SSGC story; type 8's positive sponsor thesis rests on ASL despite adverse broader evidence. Both are hypotheses, not trade permissions.

Type 7's payment, timing, partial-acceptance and failure analysis is usable. Its one-case subtypes do not validate “buy the first unpriced step”; an offer is no guaranteed floor. Many examples were not KMI-eligible.

Type 9 is an exclusion: its evidence argues against entry, yet the reflex allocates it money. Small size gives a Rs50k account no edge; Rs500k compounds thin-name exit problems. Wide stops on correlated bets, locked exits and concentrated event positions threaten either account.

**(c) Three corrections before trading.**

1. Replace the “odds” column with properly matched evidence. Freeze observable definitions, base dates, filing timestamps and next executable entries. Evaluate every eligible signal, including failures, with untouched periods and results separated by regime. Report n, mean, median, win rate and worst case after costs. Until then, label positive entry rules as research hypotheses; delete type 9's entry permission.
2. Freeze exits before entry and compare a small, predetermined set across the full candidate population. Never widen a stop because a new dip needs accommodating. Test re-entry separately. Remove automatic replacement of the holding furthest into its move: it has no supporting evidence and can sell the eventual compounder.
3. Replace 12% account risk per idea with an explicit, substantially smaller loss budget and an aggregate limit for correlated positions. The 1.5-stop gap assumption is not a loss ceiling. Stress consecutive locked sessions, absent bids and collapsed turnover; size from stressed exit capacity. Treat these limits as risk policy, not estimated optimal parameters.

**(d) Missing practitioner check:** can reported profit become cash without another financing round? Check receivables, operating cash flow, working-capital needs, debt maturities and prospective dilution before declaring an earnings inflection. Distinguish regulatory entitlement from cash collection. The catalogue mentions rights-issue disasters; funding belongs in the entry decision.
