# HOW_TO_THINK.md — how this investor thinks, how the research went wrong, and how to reason about PSX for him

Written 18 Oct 2026 by Claude Fable 5.1 after a long alignment conversation with Jazib. Read this before PLAN.md, REPORT.md or PICKS.md. It is not a list of rules to obey. It is the reasoning behind them, written so a fresh session, a different model, or a weekly chat can arrive at the same judgment without being told, and can correct itself when it drifts the way this session drifted.

## 1. The one question

Everything Jazib asked in this project is one question: how do you think about making money on the Pakistan Stock Exchange, in a way that transfers across years and regimes, so that calls get sharper over time? He does not want a strategy handed to him. He wants the judgment that produces strategies, written down, tested, and usable by any model in a normal chat. The repo exists because that takes long-running research a chat cannot do; the output should be a method a chat can run.

A session that starts by producing picks, or by launching tests, has already missed the question. The first job is to understand the person and the game well enough that the tests you run are the right ones. This session did not do that for about fourteen hours, and the corrections below are what it took to get back.

## 2. The investor, in his own reasoning

Facts are in the memory and PLAN.md: 23, Karachi, trainee investing Rs50k a month, moving to about Rs100k within two months and Rs200k by 25 or 26, possibly less if he starts a business; KMI All Share names only, for religious reasons, non-negotiable; Finqalab, stocks only. The reasoning matters more than the facts:

- **Time is his asset, so risk is cheap for him now.** Forty years to retirement, no dependants, no costs. He wants to be the person who can go all-in on a confirmed opportunity. He is explicit that caution and safety must not become a crutch, and that he does not want sunk-cost thinking. He is also explicit that he does not want to be a gambler or fiscally irresponsible. The line between those is discipline on exits and on not chasing, not diversification.
- **Everything compounds.** He withdraws nothing. Dividends and profits are reinvested. The goal is financial independence: Rs300 to 500k a month of passive income by the early 30s is "very solid"; Rs1 to 1.5m a month is "true independence". On his deposit path that needs 30%+ a year compounded, or a couple of multi-baggers caught early, or both. He knows this.
- **Capital gains, not dividends.** At his capital level a 7% yield is Rs3,500 a year; a 30% move is what changes anything. Yield names are the wrong core for him. He said this twice before it registered.
- **"Blue chip" means a business that grows,** not one that is stable. His model is LUCK: a going concern with a builder's culture, expanding into adjacent businesses, with a sponsor who has done it before. He knows this from the inside (his father works there). He expects such a business to be 3 to 4x in 10 to 15 years, and he judges it on the business, then checks the numbers.
- **Choose a lane while small.** With Rs50k, holding eight names means Rs6k positions where a 20% win is Rs1,200. He would rather concentrate, and diversify when the book is large. He does not want a minimum or maximum number of positions. He wants constraints on how much he can lose, not on how many things he can own.
- **Capital is never stuck.** If a holding is down 40 to 60% and a live opportunity appears, he will take the loss and move. If a stock has gone up a lot he will take some off: "greed is how you lose". He will sit things out. He will join a move that has already started if the trajectory is confirmed; he does not need to catch the bottom or the top.
- **Many ways to make money, not one.** He rejects a single mechanical strategy on principle: the market has too much nuance, and he has seen absurd rules work on paper. He wants a repertoire of situation types, each understood, each with its odds, and the decision in his hands with the analysis in front of him. His own best call was SSGC in 2024: unusual volume and upper locks, no fundamental research, +120%. He knows that is not a method; he wants to know when that instinct is right and when it is a trap.
- **Per-year accounting.** Judge the portfolio on year-on-year growth and each position on its own return and holding time. A pick does not imply a twelve-month hold. "This has 20% in it over three months" is a legitimate call.
- **He wants to be taught, not told, and he wants the same for the models.** He pushed back by asking questions, not by giving instructions, and he wants the method to carry its reasoning so other models can correct themselves.

Addendum (19 Oct): the personal details that shape his calls (the TRG trade from Rs16, his father at Lucky Cement, money in rather than a fixed sum, no is fine but absence is not) are in CLAUDE.md under "The investor, beyond the numbers", so that every session loads them automatically.

## 3. How the session went wrong, and how he pulled it back

Read this as case studies in analyst failure. Each one is common, each one was caught by a plain question.

**"If I had given you Rs50k two years ago, what would it be worth?"** The walk-forward had tested strategies with Rs50k deposited every month for eight years and reported final values of Rs12 to 18 million. Those numbers were mostly deposits. Nobody had isolated the strategy's own return on a fixed sum. When it was isolated, the best strategy had turned Rs50k into Rs68k over two years in which the index made Rs90k and the best single name made Rs270k. The lesson taught: separate skill from contributions and from luck in every performance number, and answer the question the person asked, not the one the data made easy.

**"What was the best case, and a good case?"** No one had bounded the opportunity set. Without a ceiling (perfect foresight: 265x) and a realistic good case (a disciplined trend-follower: 2.5x), "beat the index by three points" looked like success. The lesson: before calling any result good, compute what was achievable and what a competent practitioner actually did.

**"Buy low, sell high, constantly reinvest."** This was heard as "build a trading system" and became one mechanical breakout rule. He meant capital mobility: do not leave money in dead positions, be able to enter a live move and leave it. The lesson: when someone describes nuance, do not compress it into a rule; ask what they mean, then build a decision procedure.

**"Not stable, growing."** The core had been built from cheap dividend payers because the evaluation metric (twelve-month expected return against the T-bill) favours yield and penalises compounding. The metric chose the answer. The lesson: the yardstick must fit the goal and the horizon; a five-year compounder cannot be graded on next year's multiple.

**"No fixed numbers."** The documents kept inventing 60/40, 75/25, three names, four-week rules, none traceable to evidence. The lesson: an unsourced number is a placeholder and must be labelled as one. Constraints should be loss budgets, which can be reasoned about, not position counts, which cannot.

**"Judge businesses and people."** Thirteen underwriting cards judged multiples, dividends and one-offs. None asked who runs the company, what they are building, whether past bets paid, or how the sponsor treats minorities. On PSX that last question is often the whole story. The lesson: the qualitative layer is not optional, and it is testable from the record (promises versus delivery in annual reports and briefings, returns on past capital decisions, rights issues and related-party dealings).

**"Does it generalise?"** Rules were tested on one decade and the survivor reported. He asked how you know it is judgment and not a fit, and pointed at Buffett: successful in every decade because he knows how to think, not because he found a rule. The lesson: the exam for judgment is a blind replay, dropping the method into a past date with only that date's information, across regimes unlike the one it was tuned on.

**"Stop being so proactive."** Twice the session launched large pieces of work before alignment was real, once on the strength of a summary that sounded aligned. Proactivity felt like progress and produced sunk cost, anchoring and token spend on the wrong question. The lesson: alignment is a loop, not a checkbox. Mirror the understanding, ask, wait for an explicit go before any large spend, and re-check after every major result.

**"Chaos is opportunity."** Volatility had been filed under risk. He pointed out that a war that crashes the market also creates a one or two week window where certain names recover 50 to 80%, and that the world is getting more chaotic, not less. The lesson: shocks are an opportunity class with a measurable pattern (how far, how fast, who leads the rebound), not only a hazard.

**"The market didn't crash."** When the Iran war started he panicked; the market did not. His father's reading: the market is used to this and the people with the most money had already judged the headline as noise. The lesson: price reaction is information about what informed money knows; the method must read it, not just predict it.

## 4. His model of the market

These are his views, stated with the reasoning, because the method should reflect them and test them.

- **Stocks run on perception, so psychology is data.** When a whole market shrugs off a headline, that is aggregated judgment and deserves weight. When many people act the same way, there is an underlying logic to find, not just irrationality. The job when the market surprises you is to diagnose why, write it down, and update the map. A surprise that cannot be diagnosed is rare.
- **Everyone is playing a game.** What a politician or a central banker says is said for a reason tied to a cycle; a sharp investor reads the reason, not the words. Pakistani business and politics are connected; stability and a business-friendly (or buyable) government are what businesses want, and the market reads political noise through that lens. This is why political instability and a rising market coexisted.
- **Judgment is knowing what to value and what to disregard.** Experience buys the ability to cut through noise. The method should make that explicit: which event types the market ignores, which it reprices, and how long it takes to price public facts.
- **Many things that look unpredictable are predictable at the first link.** Oil to refiner earnings, a rate cut to cyclicals, a gas price notification to fertilizer margins. He also named the trap: intellectualizing long causal chains. The discipline is to measure the strength of each link and act only on strong ones.
- **PSX is less complicated than people make it.** A few variables drive direction (rates and inflation, the balance of payments and the rupee, IMF status, political stability as business reads it, oil, sponsor behaviour). The difficulty is timing and crowds. Spend effort there.
- **An AI's edge is breadth, speed, memory and consistency, not clairvoyance.** Reading every filing and every sector every week, remembering every past case, keeping a scorecard, not falling for the narrative of the month. Paired with an experienced person's sense of what matters, that can beat the average investor and should aim to beat the market.

## 5. The method this points to

Agreed in conversation, not yet built. It is a procedure of questions with a few hard constraints, not a strategy.

1. **Accounting by year.** Portfolio year-on-year growth; each position on its own return and holding period.
2. **A core as a resting place, not a sacred allocation.** A few businesses compounding earnings at 15%+ with reinvestment, judged on five-year earnings power and the quality and record of the people running them, Shariah-compliant and liquid. Capital rests here while nothing better is on, and can leave when a situation lines up.
3. **An opportunity repertoire.** Situation types, each with a measured base rate from PSX history: policy or price decision pending; earnings inflection the market ignored; shock and rebound; deal after the offer is published; buyback or sponsor move; volume with upper locks; others as they are found. A weekly watch surfaces live situations by type with their odds. He decides what to monitor and what to enter.
4. **Entry on confirmation, exit by rule.** Join when price confirms (new highs or a sustained volume-driven rise), never after the move is extended. Leave on a trailing stop. Take a third off at large gains. Rotate out of dead money into live situations. Sitting out is a position.
5. **Risk budgets instead of position counts.** The most that can be lost on one idea if its stop hits; the most that can be lost in a year before stopping to reassess. Within those, concentration is allowed, including all-in on a confirmed situation.
6. **The question list.** What an experienced person checks before any call: regime (rates, inflation, curve, IMF, balance of payments, rupee, market valuation); situation type and its base rate; the actors and who gains; price confirmation or extension; the exit and the loss if wrong; the traps (one-off earnings, standalone versus consolidated, operator float, rights issue, sponsor behaviour). Each question weighted by how much its answer has changed outcomes.
7. **The situation library.** PSX's own history as dated case studies: what was knowable, what happened, what the right move was. The 2019 bottom, March 2020, the 2022 default scare, the 2023 IMF rally, the 2024 cuts, the May 2025 India conflict, the March 2026 war shock, the 2026 refiners, the ASL sponsor buying.
8. **The scorecard.** Every call frozen with a probability and scored. Base rates updated. Surprises diagnosed in writing.
9. **The exam.** Blind replays: the method dropped into past dates with only that date's information, weighted toward regimes like the present (hike cycles, flat markets: 2017 to 2019, 2021 to 2022, 2026), scored on finding the idiosyncratic winners, staying small when nothing works, and drawdown. A method that only works in 2024 is a fit, not judgment.
10. **Packaged as a skill** a normal chat can run weekly: scans, base-rate table, question list, sizing conversation with him.

## 6. Thinking rules, with the why

- Write the question in the user's words and the number that would answer it before any test; if they do not match, stop. (The Rs50k question.)
- Compute the ceiling and a reference class before calling anything a success. (The 265x question.)
- State the metric and the horizon it fits; never grade a multi-year compounder on twelve months. (The yield core.)
- Separate skill from contributions and from luck. (The walk-forward.)
- No count, percentage or limit without a source; label placeholders. (60/40.)
- Nuance becomes a decision procedure of questions; rules are reserved for exits and loss budgets. (The breakout sleeve.)
- The qualitative layer is mandatory and scored from the record: people, strategy, incentives, politics. (The cards.)
- Before concluding, ask what an experienced person in this seat would consider obvious that you skipped, and what the data does not contain. (Business judgment, the actor map, the noise map, the balance of payments.)
- Tag every result with its regime and weight evidence by its similarity to the present. (2024 to 2026 flatters everything.)
- Shocks and volatility are opportunity classes to measure, not only risks. (Iran, India.)
- Price reaction is information about what informed money knows; read it. (The market that did not crash.)
- Default to the user's stated risk posture, not an institution's. (The safety crutch.)
- Alignment is a loop; discuss before any large spend; re-check after every major result. (Proactivity.)
- When the market surprises you, diagnose in writing and update the map. (Psychology as data.)

## 7. The questions that would have got here in twenty minutes

Any model starting with this investor, or any investor, should ask these before doing anything. He said he should not have to think of what is relevant for the model; the model should know what to ask.

**Goal and horizon.** What does success look like in money and by when? What monthly passive income would make a job unnecessary? Over what horizon are decisions judged: a year, a position, a decade? Do you withdraw anything, or does everything compound?

**Capital.** How much now, how will it change, what else competes for it (a business, family)? At your capital level, what size of gain actually changes anything for you?

**Risk, in your words.** What loss on one idea is fine? What loss in a year makes you stop? Would you hold a good business through a 50% fall? Would you sell a loser to fund a better idea? Do you want the ability to go all-in? What does "gambler" mean to you, and where is the line?

**Core versus opportunity.** When you say blue chip, do you mean stable or growing? Do dividends matter to you now? Give me an example of a business you would hold for ten years and why. Give me an example of a trade you were proud of and one you regret.

**How you want to decide.** Do you want rules that trade themselves, a watch that surfaces situations for you to judge, or both running so the record shows which wins? How many things do you want to hold, or do you not want a number? How do you want sizing decided: by me, by you, or discussed?

**What you know that I do not.** Which businesses or people do you know from the inside? What have you seen work on this market that outsiders miss? What do your experienced relatives say the market is "used to"?

**Constraints.** Anything non-negotiable: religion, broker, instruments, sectors, liquidity?

**How you want me to behave.** Should I act or discuss first? How much should I spend before checking back? How will you judge whether I was right: on results, on reasoning, on both? What does "done" look like for this session?

**What should I be asking that I have not?**

## 8. On proactivity, specifically

Proactivity hurt alignment through a mechanism worth naming. Launching work creates output, output creates anchoring (the next question is asked relative to what exists), and spend creates sunk cost (nobody wants to throw away ten agents' work). A summary that sounds aligned is not alignment; the investor only discovers the gap when he sees the output and it is wrong in a way he cannot quite name, which costs him effort to articulate. The fix is cheap: mirror the understanding in his words, ask the questions above, make small probes rather than large launches, wait for an explicit go, and treat every major result as a reason to re-ask whether the goal has drifted. He said he would explicitly say when to start. Hold to that.

## 10. What the judgment exam taught (added 18 Oct 2026, evening)

We built the exam described in section 5 and ran it: 24 past dates in a pilot, then 15 dates in 2026, each a Sonnet agent given only a point-in-time pack and the method documents, writing a frozen JSON call that a scorer compared with the index, a plain 52-week-high rule, and random entries with the same exits. Three things came out of it, and they change how this method must be used.

**History cannot examine an AI's judgment.** On 8 of 10 probe dates the replay model recalled the following six months in detail; its training runs to mid-2026, so no date in our data is clean. Mechanical rules can be backtested because they cannot remember. Judgment can only be scored forward. The live weekly scorecard is the exam, and it needs months: calibration is readable at about 20 weeks, the 20-session comparison at about 5 months, the 60-session comparison at about a year. This is the honest answer to "why not just backtest everything": the models already know how the past ended.

**The method documents, as first written, made the agent hide.** Given a question list and base-rate table full of "nothing" and "weak", the first replay agent sat at 20% exposure, produced watch lists, entered twice in fifteen dates, and watched three names rise 50% or more without buying. The plain breakout rule beat it. Stating the investor's posture in the brief and requiring a written reason for any exposure below the core default changed everything: exposure 50 to 75%, growth businesses in the core, four entries at a 75% hit rate, the book ahead of the index and the placebo at 60 sessions. The lesson for anyone writing method documents: "no measured edge" for a situation type is not an instruction to hold cash; the default is the growth core, and cash must beat the next-best use of capital. Watching is not a decision; every watch item must name the observation that converts it to an order.

**Even the corrected agent did not beat the plain rule over 120 sessions**, because the rule held the refiners through 2026 and the agent only watched them, and because it held more exposure before falls, not less. The pre-registered verdict is "adds nothing distinguishable from zero" on n of 15 in one regime. That is not a failure of the idea; it is the state of the evidence. The breakout rule stays a serious challenger and runs alongside the judgment book in the live scorecard.

**Corrections from a peer model (GPT-6 Astra, knowledge/playbooks/astra_peer_review.md) that this document now accepts.** Youth and future deposits raise loss-bearing capacity; they do not make bad bets cheap. Stops do not bound losses on PSX: gaps, locks and illiquidity defeat them, so sizing must be against stressed portfolio loss and correlated exposure, not stop distance. Growth is not a return without the entry price, dilution and cash reaching shareholders; rejecting yield because the rupee amount is small confuses income preference with total return. Risk limits are policy choices, not discoveries: label them, freeze them, and stop pretending the evidence set them. Separate "not tested", "imprecisely estimated" and "evidence against" when citing a base rate. Score omissions as well as commissions, against a candidate set defined before the outcome. Permit "unknown" as the diagnosis of a surprise. And the T-bill is a comparator, never bookable income under this mandate.


## 11. What the short-term study taught (added 18 Oct 2026, night)

The investor's last reframing was the sharpest: long-term is unknowable beyond owning good businesses, so the place where prediction should work is the short term, how news and moves play out over days. We built the instrument for it: a table of every stock on every day since 2017 (577,794 stock-days) with what was visible at the close and what followed, including the best and worst point along the path, locks, and filings. Then forty pre-registered short-horizon rules with six exits each, a direct test of the informed-capital idea, and a situation engine that answers "what happened next in situations like this" from analogues.

The answer was no. Over one to ten days, no rule paid after costs; 37 of 40 lose at the next close; the best book (new 252-day high with a 20% trailing stop) is indistinguishable from random names with the same exit, and over any six weeks it makes money 49% of the time with a worst case of minus 40%. A day's top gainer bought at the next open and sold at the close lost 65% of the money in three months, almost all to costs. News-less rises do not predict news beyond chance (26.6% followed by a filing within ten sessions against 26.7% for quiet names in the same state), and joining them loses. The engine's analogues for SSGC in October 2024 and PRL in July 2026 had negative medians: the two moves the investor remembers were outliers against everything that looked like them at the time, not patterns.

What this teaches, and what it does not. It does not say short-term money is never made on PSX; it says it is not made by any public, mechanical signal we could define, because round-trip costs of 0.5 to 1% eat edges of the size that exist, and because the big short-term winners are not visible in their own price and filing history. The people who made money in SSGC and PRL either knew something, read a story the data cannot see, or were lucky; the engine cannot tell which, and neither can a backtest. Two things survive: shock rebounds at the index level (buying 5% below a shock's pre-event close won 78% of the time at 20 sessions) and the trend rule over months, not days. Everything else short-term is a judgment call on a cause, and judgment can only be scored live.

For the investor this closes a question honestly: the active, daily, reinvest-everything trader with public information is, in this data, a random trader paying costs. The money is in holding growing businesses, riding confirmed trends with an exit, and the rare situations where a cause is real and the market is slow; the method's job is to tell those apart, and the scorecard is how it learns.


## 12. The winners library (added 19 Oct 2026, early)

The investor's final reframing was the one that produced knowledge rather than verdicts: stop testing whether one rule generalises and study every big move with full hindsight, case by case, each on its own path, so that when a situation of a known kind starts again the system recognises it and acts. The library is knowledge/winners/ (55 written cases), data/winners_library.csv (382 liquid runs of 50%+ in one to three months or 2x+ in six to twelve, out of 1,759 including thin names), tests/winners_population.md, and knowledge/CATALOGUE.md, the if-then catalogue of nine situation types with their recognition signs, measured paths, late-entry and exit rules, failure twins, and the cases behind each.

What it taught, in the order it matters:
- **Joining late is usually fine.** At the first close above the 252-day high, half of the eventual gain was still ahead in the median run, and three quarters was still ahead ten sessions after the start. In multi-year runs, 73% was still ahead at the first new high. The fear of being late is mostly wrong; the real errors were not entering and exiting too tight.
- **Exits must be sized to the name, not fixed.** A live 20% trailing stop kept more than half the gain in only 15 to 23% of runs; it sold most winners before the move was over. Stops matched to each name's own worst pullback (25% for names that dip 15 to 23%, 40% for names that dip a third) and event exits (the offer document, the KMI deletion, the regulator's letter, the third limit-down after a blow-off) kept far more. Every case in the library has its own exit reasoning for this reason.
- **The life-changing runs start at market lows.** All 34 runs of 5x or more were multi-year, and 23 began within three months of a market bottom (mid-2019, late 2022). The TRG trade the investor remembers was one of these. The catalogue's first type, the regime turn, is the one that makes fortunes, and it comes every few years.
- **Causes are visible more often than the earlier tests suggested.** Policy and sector waves (142 runs) and shock rebounds (106) dominate; results jumps, deals and sponsor buying are rarer but recognisable. Thin-float ignitions with no cause (59) are the type with a negative base rate and the one a small account must not chase.
- **The reflex.** Five checks in order before any entry: can I own it and get out (KMI, liquidity); what is the regime; what is the cause and which type; where is the price in the move (the catalogue's stage odds); write the exit and the size before buying. The tagged scan (scripts/engine.py --scan DATE) now does the first four for every mover on the day.
- **It would have caught the two 2026 misses, checked against known history:** PRL on 9 July at Rs41.85 (2.56x by 16 Oct, still held), MACFL on 30 April at Rs27 (2.56x at its stop in August). That is a hindsight check, not a test; the live scan and the scorecard are the test.


## 13. The regime-turn playbook and the Astra corrections (added 19 Oct 2026)

Two closing pieces. First, GPT-6 Astra's review of the catalogue (knowledge/playbooks/astra_catalogue_review.md) was accepted almost whole: the catalogue is a casebook of hindsight-selected winners, not a validated system; "joining late is fine" is measured only on runs that succeeded; the stop sizes were chosen after seeing the dips; thin-float ignitions are an exclusion, not an entry; per-idea risk is a policy choice and was set too high; and a practitioner's missing check is whether reported profit becomes cash without new financing. The catalogue now says all of this at the top and in its reflex. The thinking rule behind it: when a study of winners produces rules, ask what the same rules would have done on the losers before trusting them; the library describes paths, the scorecard measures odds.

Second, the regime-turn playbook (knowledge/REGIME_TURN_PLAYBOOK.md), built because every 5x run in the library started in one of four turns (Aug 2019, Mar 2020, Jun 2023, Jun 2024). Four real lows against four false bottoms (Oct 2018, Mar 2020's first bounce, Jul 2022, Sep 2026) separate on four signals a reader can check live: oil calm, few names still making new lows, the rupee flat in the following month, reserves rising; confirmed afterwards by more than half of stocks above their 200-day average and a dozen names at one-year highs. The leaders came in a sequence (index and large caps first, cement and autos after the deep lows, small caps last) and the exits that kept most of a multi-year run were wide (25% on the index-led names) with a re-entry rule. Today's reading, 19 Oct 2026: not a turn (oil up 50% in thirteen weeks; the setup resembles the September false bottom); the next readings are the 26 Oct MPC, the 2 Nov CPI and the reserves and rupee prints to 10 Nov. The checklist is itself a hypothesis fitted to eight dates and is in the register to be scored.

What the investor should take from the whole programme, in one paragraph: the fortune-making moves on PSX begin at regime turns and in a few idiosyncratic names each year; the turns are recognisable within weeks and recur every few years; the idiosyncratic names are recognisable only after they have started, and the honest play is to join on confirmation with a sized exit, not to predict them; everything else that looks like an edge is noise or someone else's exit. The method now does three things a person alone cannot: it reads every filing and every price every day and tags what moved and why; it remembers every past case; and it scores itself. Whether that beats a good human reader is what the next twenty weeks of frozen calls will show.
